Genes do not switch on smoothly

Transcription happens in bursts

The timing of those bursts depends on how DNA is folded, which proteins find their targets, and how quickly they let go.

We measure these steps directly in single cells and describe them with models simple enough to test.

Research

Four techniques we use to measure and model how genes switch on

3D chromatin tracing

Super-resolution imaging to trace chromatin folding in 3D, linking genome structure to gene activity cell by cell.

Transcriptional kinetics

How transcription factors and promoter architecture set burst frequency and size, measured in individual cells.

Stochastic models

Physical models of noisy gene expression, fit directly to imaging data to extract rates and test mechanisms.

Super-resolution methods

Super-resolution images are only as good as the analysis behind them. We develop algorithms that remove artifacts from fluorophore blinking, correct localization error, and sharpen 3D precision, so that distances measured inside single cells can be trusted.

    Dr. Christopher H. Bohrer received dual B.S. degrees in physics and science education before pursuing a Ph.D. in Biophysics and Biophysical Chemistry at Johns Hopkins University. Under the co-mentorship of Drs. Jie Xiao and Elijah Roberts, he received training in both theoretical and experimental approaches, focusing on super-resolution microscopy method development and the relationship between DNA organization and transcription regulation.

    Since joining Dr. Daniel R. Larson's group at the NIH in 2020, Christopher has investigated how chromatin organization influences transcription regulation, with particular emphasis on enhancer biology. His work has revealed fundamental principles governing how the physical distance between genes drives co-expression.

    In January, Christopher will start his independent research group as an Assistant Professor in the Department of Physics at The Ohio State University. Outside of science, Christopher has three little boys, a wife, and a dog (Galileo) who keep him busy.
