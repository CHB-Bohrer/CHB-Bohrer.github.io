The life of one gene

A five-minute story for undergraduates, prospective students and anyone curious. Scroll to follow a single gene as it switches on, fires and falls silent.

  1. 01

    Meet the gene

    Every cell in your body carries about two meters of DNA, folded into a nucleus roughly a tenth the width of a human hair. Our gene is one short stretch of it.

    Right now it is off. The DNA here is wrapped tightly around spools of protein called nucleosomes, the grey beads, and nothing is reading it.

  2. 02

    The search

    To switch the gene on, a protein called a transcription factor has to find it. Factors have no map. They wander through the nucleus, stick to DNA for a moment, let go and try again.

    Most visits last only seconds, and most are in the wrong place. Watch the highlighted factor try a few spots before it lands on the gene's switch, the promoter.

  3. 03

    Opening up

    The factor recruits helpers that loosen the packaging, and nucleosomes near the gene are cleared away. Far along the DNA, a control region called an enhancer is brought close by a loop in the fiber.

    How often enhancers and promoters actually meet, and whether meeting is enough, is one of the questions our lab measures directly in single cells.

  4. 04

    The burst

    Now the gene fires. RNA polymerase enzymes load onto the promoter one after another, each copying the gene into a messenger RNA.

    This is a burst: many copies in a short window. The count in the trace below climbs quickly.

  5. 05

    Silence

    The factor lets go and the promoter switches off. Polymerases already on the gene finish their copies, then nothing new starts. The gene can stay quiet for minutes or hours.

    Its messenger RNAs drift away to be made into protein and are slowly broken down, so the count falls.

  6. 06

    Why it is random, and why that matters

    Each step depends on a handful of molecules finding each other, so the timing is random. Two identical cells side by side can hold very different numbers of the same messenger RNA.

    That randomness is not a flaw to ignore. Cells have evolved ways to tune it, and we want to know how: we watch these steps in single cells with super-resolution microscopy and build physical models that explain the timing.

  7. 07

    When the switch breaks

    Healthy tissue depends on this timing. Genes that tell a cell to grow and divide should fire only when growth is needed, then fall quiet. Many diseases, including most cancers, involve genes switched on at the wrong time or at the wrong level.

    In Burkitt lymphoma, a cancer of immune cells, a chromosome rearrangement moves the growth gene MYC next to a powerful enhancer that normally drives antibody production. The gene fires far more often than it should, its messenger RNA piles up, and the cells keep dividing. Learning exactly how enhancers set the rhythm of bursting is how we understand what goes wrong.

    If that sounds like the kind of puzzle you want to work on, come talk to us.
